# Crisis Injection Patterns

How to add a central engineering/political crisis to transform a plotless book into a plot-driven one.

## When to Use

- Book has no central plot — chapters are independent episodes
- Rating is C+ or below with "no rising tension" as a critical weakness
- Antagonist and plot thread insertion (from the structural fix phase) needs a visible crisis to anchor to

## The Core Pattern

```
Midpoint: Chapter N where N = total_chapters // 2

The crisis follows this arc across 5-8 chapters:

Ch N:   Problem appears — visible, specific, matters to the protagonist
Ch N+1: Initial solution fails — crisis worsens
Ch N+2: New information discovered — reveals underlying cause
Ch N+3: Desperate solution attempted — partial success buys time
Ch N+4: Crisis stabilized but reveals a WORSE underlying problem
Ch N+5: Characters must choose between two bad options
Ch N+6-8: Crisis resolution — sacrifice, breakthrough, or escalation
```

## Sci-Fi Crisis Types

| Crisis | Specific Mechanism | Visible Consequences |
|--------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Life support failure | CO2 scrubber saturation, oxygen generator failure, water recycler clog | Rising CO2 levels, rationing, crew panic, protagonist running diagnostics |
| Micrometeorite strike | Hull breach in a non-critical module, cascade of micro-fractures | Depressurization alarm, emergency EVA, temporary patch that won't hold |
| Power system cascade | Solar array micro-crack, battery thermal runaway, inverter failure | Lights flickering, systems going offline one by one, backup batteries draining |
| Structural failure | Stress crack in a critical load-bearing element | Visible vibration in the module, telemetry showing metal fatigue, engineering team racing to reinforce |
| Communication loss | Antenna damage from solar flare, signal interference from debris | Cannot contact Earth for help, no updates on resupply, crew feels isolated |
| Bio-contamination | Fungal growth in water reclamation, bacterial bloom in hydroponics | Crew getting sick, food supply threatened, protagonist must solve with limited tools |

## Non-Sci-Fi Crisis Types

| Genre | Crisis | Mechanism |
|-------|--------|-----------|
| Legal/Cozy | Evidence suppressed | Key document ruled inadmissible, surprise witness contradicts the case, judge recuses |
| Business | Competitor launch | Rival product released early, investor pulls funding, regulatory deadline accelerated |
| Memoir | Loss of central figure | Caregiver dies, diagnosis arrives, relationship ends |
| Thriller | Countdown | Bomb timer, ransom deadline, extraction window closing |

## Injecting the Crisis (Step by Step)

1. **Find the midpoint lull.** Open the book around Ch16-20 in a 39-chapter book. If the chapter has no stakes, no conflict, no irreversible event — that's your target.

2. **Rename the chapter.** Change the heading to something that signals crisis: "Critical Systems Failure," "The Breach," "The Countdown Begins."

3. **Write the crisis trigger.** The problem must be:
   - **Visible:** Characters can see/hear/smell the failure
   - **Specific:** Not "something is wrong" but "the CO2 reading is 4.2% and climbing"
   - **Personal:** It matters to the protagonist specifically

4. **Write the first failure.** The crew's first attempt to fix it makes things worse. Every attempted solution reveals a deeper problem.

5. **Cascade through subsequent chapters.** Don't resolve in one chapter. Each subsequent chapter should mention the crisis — it's ticking in the background, getting worse, or the crew is working on it while handling other tasks.

6. **Tie to existing plot threads.** The crisis should connect to the antagonist or the book's political conflict. Example: the failed replacement part is blocked by Cole's lobbying, so the crew can't just order a new one.

## Before vs After Example (Mooncoming)

**Before (C+):** Ch16 was "Manufacturing Scale-Up" — a 600-word description of production targets. Read: "We increased production. It worked. Move on."

**After (B):** Ch16 became "Critical Systems Failure" — a power grid cascade that forces Tom and Maya to work together. Subsequent chapters show: the fix failing, a backup system failing too, the crew's last resort being a dangerous EVA, and finally a partial stabilization that reveals a worse underlying problem.

**Transformation:** Every chapter after Ch16 now carries the question "Can they fix it before it kills them?" instead of "What will they build next?"

## Pitfalls

- **Crisis resolving too easily.** If the fix works on the first try, there was no crisis. Always include at least one failure before the solution.
- **Crisis disconnected from character stakes.** The crisis should be personal to the protagonist, not an abstract systems failure.
- **Crisis forgotten in later chapters.** Once introduced, the crisis must be referenced or felt in every subsequent chapter until resolved.
- **Crisis overshadows the main plot.** If the crisis IS the ending, it's not a crisis — it's the climax. A crisis should raise stakes, not resolve them.
